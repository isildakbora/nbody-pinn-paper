Time-Marching Physics-Informed Neural Networks for the Planar Three-Body Problem

A neural network is trained only on Newton's equations of motion, with no trajectory data, and reproduces a figure-eight orbit to a maximum error of 3×10-4 over one period. The animations below let you inspect the orbit, the method, the errors, and where the method breaks down.

Problem

Newton's equations, three bodies

Each body feels the pull of the other two. With unit gravitational constant, the planar motion is a system of second-order ODEs. Its solution is not closed-form in general, and generic orbits are chaotic. A PINN has to satisfy these equations everywhere on its time domain.

d2xi/dt2 = ∑j≠i mj (xj − xi) / ‖xj − xi‖3,   i = 1, 2, 3

Drag the slider to see the residual of this equation for the PINN solution. The acceleration on the left is a finite-difference derivative of the predicted velocities, and the one on the right is computed from the predicted positions. The network is trained to drive their difference to zero.

The residual is evaluated on the exported PINN samples, so the finite difference adds a small discretisation error of its own.
Method

Build the initial state into the network

The network does not learn the initial state from a loss term. It is built into the ansatz, so it holds exactly at every training step:

xθ(t) = x0 + v0 t + t2 Nθ(t)

The term x0 + v0 t is the free-motion line that the initial state implies. The network only learns the correction t2N. Switch between methods below to compare their trajectories with the reference. Each panel shows one body, which is enough to see the failure modes.

The PINN draws the path itself; the baselines are the same ODE solved with the alternatives. The soft-IC network and the monolithic hard-IC network both fail to represent the full period with a single network.
Method

Time marching

A single network over the whole period has to represent a curve whose equations only constrain it locally, and errors accumulate along the way. The fix is to cut time into segments. Each segment gets its own small network, and it starts from the state that the previous segment predicts at its end. The handoff is exact, so the trajectory is continuous by construction.

Validation

Energy is conserved, to a point

The total energy of an isolated system is constant. The PINN solution does not enforce this, but it can be checked from its positions and velocities. The chart shows the difference from the initial energy along the figure-eight.

Limitation

The network does not extrapolate

Each segment is trained only on its own time window, so the solution is reliable on [0,T] and nowhere else. The slider runs through the second period, which the network has never seen. The readout turns red once time passes T.

Generality

Other initial conditions

The same method was run on an equal-mass Lagrange triangle and on a random, non-periodic initial condition over a horizon of two time units. Select a case to see its trajectories.

Scope

What this does and does not show

Validated: one period of the figure-eight and of the Lagrange triangle, and a random initial condition over [0, 2], each against an independent high-accuracy solver. The energy drift stays at the 10-4 level.
Not validated: generalisation over initial conditions (each orbit is trained from scratch, about 12 minutes), chaotic long-horizon orbits, close encounters, and unequal masses.
Not built: the parametric network Nθ(t, y0) that would generalise across initial conditions. It is the natural next step.
